Sense about Science and the Policy Institute at King’s College London, with funding from Wellcome, [surveyed 4,262 people across the UK on their levels of trust in scientists](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/research/trust-in-scientists-the-role-of-values-and-moral-and-political-identities-in-the-uk). The results show that nearly two-thirds (63%) of people have high trust in scientists.

One of the biggest drivers of public trust in scientists is whether people think of scientists as being on their side. It is more important to the public to view scientists as “one of them” with either working in the science sector or personally knowing someone that does, being strongly associated with high levels of trust. The perception of shared values and identity is valued above how knowledgeable or “expert” people considered scientists to be. Therefore, belief that the interests of scientists and the public align and that scientists are acting in the publics’ best interests is an important foundation of trust. Trust is also strongly associated with people viewing scientists as being open and transparent, highlighting the importance of clear and accurate communication of research. Being honest about complexities, nuances and uncertainties in research, rather than trying to oversimplify scientific findings is key to building and maintaining trust.

Only a small minority of the public have high levels of distrust in scientists in general (5%). However, the level of distrust varies across scientific fields. Climate scientists are the least trusted group with 30% of people stating they’re suspicious of climate researchers. Furthermore, some distrustful beliefs are quite widespread: 46% of the public believe that scientists wouldn’t publish results if they thought their funders wouldn’t like them. Distrust also had a strong political correlation, with only 32% of Reform UK supporters viewing scientists as on their side compared to 71% of Green supporters. These political associations suggest there is a risk trust or distrust in science may become politically divided, making addressing distrust a more complex process.

Public trust in science is important as it influences the impact scientific findings can have on people’s lives. For example, 71% of people with high trust in scientists agree government decisions and policies should be informed by scientific guidance compared to just 30% of people with middling trust. Trust also strongly influences whether people follow scientific advice. 42% of people with low trust ignore any information from scientists with this figure being higher concerning advice from vaccine and climate scientists specifically. For example, 88% of people with high trust in vaccine scientists say they would get a recommended vaccine compared to only 16% of people with low trust. Similarly, only 6% of people with low trust in climate scientists say scientific evidence of climate change influences their behaviour in contrast to 69% of high trusters. Furthermore, people who trust scientists are also more likely to support public funding of science. Of people with low trust in scientists, 34% want public funding of science cut compared to 2% of people with high trust, highlighting how scientific advancement itself can depend on the trust people have in scientists.

While two-thirds of the public trust scientists, distrust is still circling and in some groups in society distrust is high. So how can we work towards strengthening trust in science? The public say the top actions to improve trust include scientists actively correcting misinformation and explaining how research can positively impact the lives of ordinary people. Meanwhile the actions that are often used to try and improve trust, such as scientists advising policymakers, were much less popular with the public.

Promisingly, only a very small proportion of people say there is nothing scientists can do to improve levels of trust, so it seems that in an era of mistrust and misinformation, trust in science is still possible and achievable.
